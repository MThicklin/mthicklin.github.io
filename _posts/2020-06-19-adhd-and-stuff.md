<p class="has-text-align-center">-OR-<br>How I learned to stop worrying and not impulsively buy stuff.</p>

<p>As I learn more about what ADHD actually is (Turns out it's more then just 'can't pay attention'); I'm given more insight into myself and why my mind processes the way it does.  One aspect I learned to mostly control early on is the tendency to impulsively buy or collect stuff.</p>

<p>We all have that one thing we can convince ourselves to buy.  My weakness has consistently been books.  Buying books makes me feel like I'm make progress towards something, even if I don't open it.  When I'm ready, I have the book available to me, so that's progress right?  Small victories and all that?  But each impulsive book purchase made me realize the stack of other impulsive book purchases wasn't getting any smaller.  </p>

<p>No matter, THIS book; This was the ONE.  The one I would hunker down and finish and really take the lessons to heart and change my ways.  I would do good for the first few days, I would get a few pages in everyday.  But soon, something would pull my attention.  A new project pops in my head, I would skip a few days doing other things or maybe the book turns out to be dry or boring.</p>

<p>Psychologically, there is positive effect of buying stuff.  But like most positive things, people (This includes everybody, this isn't just a ADHD exclusive thing; It hits differently for everyone) tend to become more dependent on it and leads us to buying stuff for that dopamine hit. </p>

<p>It usually starts with the justification... It could be anything: Starting a project, adding to a collection or just wanting something.  That excitement hits a peak and we make the purchase.  </p>

<p>Then reality sets in.  Maybe that project is going to take longer then you thought, maybe that collection needs one more item, or that thing you wanted wasn't want you though it was going to be.  Soon, that item gets added to the pile to become 'out of sight, out of mind'.  </p>

<p>I still fall prey to my 'good' intentions when buying things.  The difference now is I'm getting better.  I'm having actual small victories instead of the false victories.  I'm learning more about myself and the tricks my brain tries to pull to get dopamine.  I still come across a cool project using a raspberry pi.  I find an online class that promises to show me how to finally get that program I have in my head off the ground or maybe a book caught my eye and could really change my life.  </p>

<p>What can we do in a situation like this?  When these situations come up, we stop and think.  Don't I have a google doc full of ideas I need to work on?  Don't I have other classes I need to find before I get to this one? I have my kindle here, I'll download a sample of the book and see if it's really what I'm looking for.  Using this system we get back into perspective and remember to finish what's on my plate before we pile more on.</p>

<p>My habit of having too much on my plate only to seek out more isn't multi-tasking or being productive in general.  I'm not too sure what you would call it... task-hopping?  Whatever it is, it only produces half-started projects and leads me to feel like I'm a failure.  Usually it's not the actual project that generates the excitement, it's the 'starting' of something new.</p>

<p>When I get the urge to buy something, I now view it as an exercise in self-control.  If I'm in the process of doing something that will warrant getting a reward, I will take note of the thing and work towards it.  Sometimes I take note of the item and unintentionally forget about it.   To overly paraphrase the old adage, 'If you want something... let it go.  If you desire it after reaching a goal, make it yours.  If you forget about it, you probably didn't really wanted it in the first place.'</p>

<p>You might be thinking, "OK, K3ntucky, How do I fix this?".  You can't... At least not in a permanent, one-size-fits-all, never-worry-about-it-again way.  Some of us buy an impulsive piece of candy every once in a while with no detrimental side effects, Some of us show no signs but wake up one day with 20 packages of useless gadgets hoping one of them serves the purpose giving us what we are looking for.</p>

<p>A good first step is to acknowledge and accept our desire to change but also fail-proof ourselves.  I don't want you to label failures with "You just didn't WANT to change bad enough".  This cheapens the times you really wanted something but couldn't quite hit the mark.  The fact that you tried is a valiant effort and shouldn't be thrown out as simply a failure.</p>

<p>A good next step is to recognize our thinking patterns around impulsive buys and take note of when feel that we HAVE to buy something.  Sometimes we'll catch it as it starts, sometimes we'll catch ourselves in the middle it or maybe we'll spot it as we're walking up to the register.  But if we can catch it and replace it, we have a better chance of understanding why we are buying that thing and what we can do to prevent another unneeded purchase.</p>

<p>I usually replace "I want this" / "I have to have this" thinking with:<br>Do I really need this, right now? / What is a replacement for this? / Is this really missing in my life?</p>

<p>While we must learn to respect and make peace with our internal thoughts, never discount the external enemies of distraction we battle for the hold of our attention. A wide variety of advertising algorithms specifically aimed at our incredibly niche tastes are a constant threat to hijack our senses and convince us that their product is the missing key to our lives.  But, as Sun Tzu teaches us, 'If you know your enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles.'</p>

<p>I think I heard someone in the back, "So what? That's easy for you say, you have self control."  Self control is something we will constantly work on and improve over our lives and our will power alone can only take us so far.  It's easy to fall prey to the seductive positive effects of buying things.  We must find systems that work for us and realize a slip up isn't the end, but a chance to improve the system and, in effect, ourselves.</p>
<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2>TL;DR</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->
<p><strong>Slip ups ARE NOT failures. It's only a failure if you refuse to improve afterwards.</strong></p>

<p>Replace "I have to have this" style of thinking with:<br>     Do I really need this, right now?<br>     What is a replacement for this?<br>     Is this really missing in my life?</p>

<p>Try to answer:<br>     Was I happy before I knew of the existence of this item?  Why is this item the key to my  happiness?<br>     What's a task / chore / goal this thing could be a reward for?<br>     Do I really want this item or have I been hijacked by clever advertising?</p>

<p>Self control and will power are life long battles / projects to work on.  But if the temptation is too great, try to deny yourself the battle and make 'have to buys' into rewards for tasks you need to do.</p>

<p>Everyone is different, you have to look inside and find what works for you.  Maybe it's something in this post, maybe it's a combination of things, maybe it's nothing in this post.  Life, on the individual perspective, is mostly trial and error.</p>
